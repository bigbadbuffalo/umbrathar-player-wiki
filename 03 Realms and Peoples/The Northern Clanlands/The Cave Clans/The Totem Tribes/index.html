    The Totem Tribes¶

    The Totem Tribes are a loose outsider name for related human tribes scattered through the mountains and deep wild country of the Svealish lands, Khoryv, and neighboring northern regions. No common native name is known. Individual tribes identify first by their own people, kin, territory, cave, and other inherited relationships.

    Most neighboring peoples regard the Totem Tribes as among the most primitive and frightening humans in Umbrathar. That judgment is colored by fear and prejudice, but it is not based on pure invention. The tribes preserve lifeways that seem impossibly ancient to their agricultural and metalworking neighbors: they live as nomadic or semi-nomadic hunter-gatherers, build no permanent houses, and make their only permanent settlements in natural or modified caves.

    They are nevertheless human, not a separate race or an intermediate form between humanity and Beastmen.

    Life in the Wilds¶

    Totem-Tribe communities move through mountain valleys, forests, uplands, and other wild country according to game, fish, gathered foods, seasons, and local custom. Temporary camps may use hide shelters, lean-tos, windbreaks, brush structures, or whatever the local environment provides, but these are not permanent villages.

    Caves are different. A cavern may be occupied by generations of the same people and contain hearths, storage pits, workshops, ritual chambers, burials, defensive chokepoints, smoke-blackened gathering places, and remembered spaces associated with ancestors. Some cave systems are little more than seasonal refuges; others are ancient home-centers whose occupation reaches beyond living memory.

    A great cave is rarely one undifferentiated holy place. Ordinary living chambers may be comparatively mundane while a burial gallery, an ancient hearth, a fissure, a chamber marked by generations of ritual use, a place associated with funerary consumption, or a sealed passage carries very different obligations and dangers. Some such places are genuine Places of Power created by spirits, old events, sympathy, accumulated remains, repeated fear or devotion, Vested Will, or several causes at once. The people who use them may know that a place is dangerous without knowing exactly why.

    Their material culture relies heavily on stone, bone, antler, hide, sinew, wood, and other locally available materials. Metal goods may be scavenged, captured, or traded for, but the absence of ordinary agriculture, masonry towns, and widespread metalworking is a genuine cultural difference rather than merely a southern slander.

    Animal Relationships¶

    Inherited relationships with animals are common and important among the Totem Tribes, but the outsider word totem makes them look more uniform than they are.

    A relationship may belong to a whole people, a clan, a band, a household, a ritual group, a particular cave, or several nested groups at once. Some communities maintain more than one such affiliation, some preserve only a weakened or transformed form, and a few may lack an explicit animal affiliation altogether.

    Where such a relationship exists, it is not merely an emblem or personality type. People inherit obligations they did not personally choose. Stories may describe an animal as ancestor, rescuer, teacher, dangerous kinsman, spouse, bargaining partner, spiritual double, or something less easily translated. What matters most is what the relationship requires: what may be hunted or eaten, what must be returned, which hides or bones may be used, which names or signs are restricted, which places matter, and what must be done when an unusual animal appears.

    The affiliated animal is not necessarily protected from killing. One people may avoid killing it except under dire need, another may require ritual consumption, and another may place special rules only on particular remains or exceptional animals.

    These inherited relationships also differ from personal spirit relationships. An individual may acquire a durable relationship through a dream, illness, ordeal, near-death, repeated encounter, survived possession, unusual hunt, or specialist training. Most adults do not require such a bond, and having one does not automatically make a person a public ritual specialist. A personal spirit can also conflict with inherited obligations, creating a real religious problem without automatically proving heresy or deception.

    Enough inherited animal relationships involve real spirits, old sacred places, sympathy, and accumulated Vested Will that they possess genuine supernatural weight. This does not require every Bear-affiliated people to worship the same immortal being called Bear, nor does it make every tribal explanation of the relationship literally correct.

    Taking, Sharing, and Return¶

    Totem religious life commonly treats hunting as more than the moment of killing. Taking life creates an unfinished relationship. A hunt must be made intelligible through the circumstances of taking, the distribution of what was taken, and whatever local act completes the relationship afterward.

    A kill creates claims beyond the person who struck the final blow. Meat, hide, bone, sinew, organs, trophies, prestige, and even the right to tell the story may belong to different people according to need, kinship, hunt role, status, guest obligations, or local custom. Keeping the wrong thing, denying a rightful share, destroying what should circulate, or failing to return what should cease to be human property can be understood as waste even when no usable food is thrown away.

    The final act varies sharply. A people may place selected bones in a cave, return blood to water, leave a portion to predators, burn something, speak over the remains, carry a part to an old place, or insist that complete human use is itself the proper return. There is no universal Totem ceremony.

    Not every inherited rule is objectively supernatural. Some preserve real spirit agreements, sympathetic dangers, or Vested Will; some encode hard-won practical knowledge; some are social customs; some are obsolete or simply wrong. Even skilled practitioners cannot always tell which is which before consequences appear.

    Cannibalism and the Dead¶

    Cannibalism is widespread enough among the Totem Tribes to be part of the cultural field rather than an exotic exception.

    Many tribes practice funerary cannibalism, consuming prescribed parts of their own dead as one stage in settling the person among the established dead. Human flesh, marrow, organs, blood, bones, ash, retained teeth, skulls, or other remains may pass through very different treatments depending on the tribe. What appears horrifying to outsiders may locally be understood as care for the dead and protection against leaving a kinsman's remains improperly exposed to hostile powers.

    Many tribes also consume selected portions of defeated enemies. Such consumption can be intended to partake in qualities admired in the dead—courage, strength, endurance, ferocity, cunning, or spiritual potency—and need not be an act of contempt. The rules differ sharply over who may be consumed, what portions, by whom, when, and under what circumstances.

    This does not mean every Totem Tribe treats every human body as food. The important differences lie in boundaries and obligations. A practice proper for an honored kinsman may be forbidden for a stranger; a worthy enemy may be eligible while a coward is not; some participants may be excluded; some portions may never be eaten; and some tribes impose extensive restrictions that others do not share.

    The Wyndol are feared even among peoples for whom cannibalism itself is not inherently shocking. Their notoriety rests on breadth, coercion, cruelty, and especially stories—apparently not entirely invented—of beginning to consume defeated enemies while they are still alive.

    Death itself is also understood as a process rather than only an instant. Bodily death begins a period in which relationships, remains, possessions, memory, and sometimes supernatural phenomena have not yet been fully settled. Fresh bodies, decomposing remains, clean bones, ashes, skulls, retained hair or teeth, and substitute objects can carry different statuses.

    This does not mean every dead person's Soul remains trapped until the tribe completes a rite. Souls are real, but most post-death hauntings are not surviving Souls, and a troubled death may involve residue, sympathy, old obligations, spirits, place, memory, or nothing supernatural at all. Different tribes often use one local explanation for several phenomena that learned cosmology would distinguish.

    Sacred Places, Routes, and Time¶

    Totem sacred geography includes more than caves. Hunting circuits, seasonal trails, recurring camps, burial places, affiliated-animal sites, water, old kill places, spirit-contact locations, and routes between home-centers can all acquire significance through repeated use, remembered events, spirits, danger, or Vested Will.

    Old rules can outlive their explanations. A warning never to spill blood at one crossing or never to enter a chamber during first thaw may preserve a real supernatural obligation, practical survival knowledge, an obsolete custom, or a false belief. The people obeying it may not know which.

    Sacred time is equally local. Important observances may follow prey movement, fish runs, rutting, calving, denning, plant foods, first or last snow, cave occupation, gatherings, mortuary thresholds, lunar conditions, or other recurring events. The event itself usually matters more than a numbered date: if the prey is late, the observance may be late as well.

    Ordinary people can know practical rules concerning the Moon, dreams, dangerous nights, hunting signs, or spirit contact without sharing one universal lunar theology. The objective lunar phases and the Moon's recurring spiritual Faces are both real, but individual peoples need not use the familiar Northern names or interpret them the same way.

    Ordinary Religious Life¶

    Most religion is performed by ordinary people. Children learn it through stories, work, imitation, warnings, correction, and participation: who receives which share, which place is avoided, how the dead are spoken of, what one does before departing, and what must happen when something is brought home.

    Daily religious practice is usually light but pervasive rather than ceremonially elaborate. A person may give the correct remnant away, place something where it belongs, speak a brief prayer, burn a customary substance, observe a household prohibition, acknowledge the dead, or follow a warning inherited from people whose explanation has long been forgotten.

    The Totem Tribes generally do not see a contradiction between practical and spiritual causation. Cold injures flesh because it is cold; wrapping and warming the body helps because that is what warmth does; those same facts may be understood through local relationships with Cold, Earth, Hearth, Life, animal powers, ancestors, or other spirits. A wound can be cleaned and bound while prayer, smoke, offering, or taboo observance accompanies the treatment. Different tribes explain these relationships differently, and no single elemental theology is implied.

    Birth, naming, marriage, adoption, adulthood, and other changes of status can carry religious weight, but there is no single Totem life-cycle sequence and no universal initiation or vision quest.

    Specialists, Dreams, and Spirits¶

    Totem Tribes can possess healers, dream-readers, spirit-mediators, funerary authorities, cave custodians, hunt specialists, memory-keepers, dangerous solitary ritualists, and other local specialists. Outsiders often call all of them Shamans, but there is no standardized Totem office.

    Most dreams are only dreams. A dream becomes difficult to dismiss when it repeats, proves true, conveys unusual knowledge, brings recognizable spirit contact, or produces consequences. Likewise, fasting, isolation, danger, sleeplessness, or sacred places can create an opportunity for revelation without guaranteeing one.

    Even genuine supernatural experience does not automatically confer public authority. A visionary still has to be believed; a healer earns reputation; a ritual custodian depends on precedent and social trust; and a private spirit obligation may have no authority over anyone else.

    Totem Tribes and Beastmen¶

    Southern priests and frontier storytellers often connect the Totem Tribes with Beastmen. Some Atharite writers describe them as humans on the road to degeneration; others insist that their ancient lifeways merely make them look like a missing stage between civilized humanity and true Beastmen.

    The Totem Tribes themselves remain human. True Beastmen are physically and behaviorally distinct and occur far beyond Totem-Tribe territory. Particular tribes may cooperate with Beastmen, use them in war, avoid them, hunt them, or incorporate them into local supernatural explanations without proving common ancestry.

    Outsider Reputation¶

    The Totem Tribes occupy an uncomfortable place between rumor and fact. Outsiders exaggerate them, but they do not invent everything. Some cave complexes genuinely contain large quantities of human and animal bone. Some tribes consume their own dead or selected enemies. Some practice frightening rites, display filed teeth, or leave animal skulls and other signs at territorial boundaries. Other communities may be far less hostile than their reputation suggests.

    For most travelers, the practical problem is that knowing one tribe tells you very little about the next.
