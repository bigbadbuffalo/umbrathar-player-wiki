    Asurabalah¶

    Overview¶

    Asurabalah is the dominant state and sacrificial religious order of Devi. The name is commonly understood in the sense of the strength or power of the Asura. It is explicitly Diabolist: its great cults venerate demonic powers whose institutions are intertwined with rulership, caste hierarchy, warfare, commerce, law, magical practice, and ritual sacrifice.

    Asurabalah is not a single church and does not possess one harmonized creed. The cults of the Great Demon Gods compete fiercely, preserve incompatible sacred histories, and may disagree about creation, divine rank, or the ultimate order of the cosmos. What unifies them is a shared religious and institutional grammar: offerings, vows, sacred debts, pollution, purification, sacrifice, patronage, lawful cult standing, temple privilege, and the accepted political supremacy of the Asura order.

    Nor is Asurabalah simply “the religion of Devi.” Devi religious life is older and much broader than the modern Diabolist establishment. Household gods, ancestors, local spirits, village shrines, ascetic traditions, regional festivals, protective rites, and fragments of older philosophical schools continue beneath or alongside the state cults. Most Devi therefore live within a layered religious world rather than practicing an exclusive Demon-God faith.

    The beings worshipped as Great Demon Gods are known through specifically Devi names, images, titles, and cult traditions. These are not necessarily universal true names or immutable cosmic bodies. A Demon may be known elsewhere under a different guise, while apparently similar foreign cults may instead serve entirely different beings.

    Historical Rise¶

    Asurabalah in its modern form is not the timeless religion of Devi.

    Old Devi was famously pluralistic, with innumerable temples, cults, ascetic schools, household traditions, philosophical movements, and occult specialists. Diabolism was known and periodically dangerous, and some lesser or bounded dealings with demons could even be licensed or tolerated under local law, but no civilization-wide Demon-God order ruled the country.

    The transformation began during the long invasion of Devi by the Dragon Emperor in the seventh century AA. Desperate practitioners escalated from harsh blood magic and voluntary sacrifice to captives, coercive rites, demonic enhancement, and progressively more open dealings with demons. Repeated success made each new exception easier to justify.

    At Shari, defenders finally invoked Durogchit the Skull Reaper openly as a wartime ally. She demanded captured enemy soldiers and answered with demon legions, war-beasts, supernatural endurance, battle-fury, and other unmistakable manifestations. Shari survived, and the precedent was devastatingly persuasive: a Demon God had answered when conventional institutions appeared unable to save the city.

    Other rulers and cults sought comparable patrons. The result was not an overnight conversion but generations of institutional capture, imitation, rivalry, and escalation. After the Immortal armies withdrew, the armed cults and enhanced rulers created by the war continued fighting one another until the first Asura Raj eventually consolidated the recognizable modern order.

    The name Asurabalah belongs particularly to this mature religious-political settlement. The older wartime cults, sacrificial schools, and Demon-God covenants predated the unified establishment and need not have called themselves by the later name.

    Shared Belief and Cult Rivalry¶

    At the broadest level, Asurabalah teaches that the Great Demon Gods are mighty powers with legitimate claims upon the world and mankind, and that humans may prosper by entering correctly ordered relationships with them. Beyond that proposition, agreement rapidly breaks down.

    One cult may describe its patron as first among the gods, another as the necessary enemy of a rival, and a third as the hidden power behind some ancient event. Such contradictions are ordinary. Asurabalah is held together less by theology than by shared procedures, recognized institutions, and a political settlement that permits rivalry without allowing cult warfare to destroy the state.

    The Raj stands above this religious field as supreme sacred ruler and arbiter, not as universal high priest of every Demon God. He can confirm standing, suppress destabilizing cults, recognize privileges, and intervene in disputes. A great priest may nevertheless claim—and perhaps sincerely believe—that no mortal knows a particular patron more intimately than the priesthood dedicated to that god.

    Recognition by the Asura state defines lawful Asurabalah, but outlaw cults may remain recognizably part of the same wider religious family. The cult of Dahakrodha, for example, shares much of the same ritual language while turning its violence against the established order.

    Temple Life and Predatory Generosity¶

    Major temples are not merely places of sacrifice. They may function as healers, diviners, arbitrators, lenders, archives, granaries, employers, warehouses, schools, funerary institutions, pilgrimage houses, magical protectors, and intermediaries with officials. Much of this institutional life descends from older Devi religious and civic traditions captured or transformed by the Asura order rather than invented by it.

    The services are often real. A temple healer may genuinely save a child, a diviner may identify a danger, a granary may feed a district during scarcity, and a priest may remove a hostile possession. The danger lies in the obligations created by accepting help.

    Asurabalah temples commonly practice a form of predatory generosity. Initial services may be free, subsidized, or cheap enough to invite reliance. Later, especially when a petitioner is desperate, the cost may become money, labor, land, service, political loyalty, a blood obligation, a family commitment, or a sacred debt that can be invoked years later. Temples therefore cultivate clients as much as they collect fees.

    This makes temple patronage simultaneously useful and dangerous. Families learn which shrines are generous, which priests are notorious for converting favors into lifelong claims, when relatives can pool resources to avoid temple debt, and when one cult can be used to contest the demands of another.

    Offerings, Sacred Debt, and Sacrifice¶

    Asurabalah recognizes many forms of offering. Food, incense, animals, valuables, labor, vows, fasting, bloodletting, ordeal, festival gifts, and temple service are all more common in ordinary religious life than human sacrifice.

    Human sacrifice is nevertheless normalized as an institution. Major temples, rulers, armies, and courts maintain the officials, guards, records, captive populations, ritual precincts, supplies, and schedules necessary to conduct it. Judicial sacrifice, war captives, great state rites, elite vows, and extraordinary crises provide common contexts, though exact rules differ sharply by cult and Rajdom.

    Sacred debt connects ordinary religious dependence to the coercive order without making every debtor a sacrificial victim. Debt may first result in labor, clientage, loss of property, temple service, hostage obligations, political duties, enslavement, or family liability. Only an additional legal or religious classification normally makes a person eligible for sacrifice.

    Asurabalah law commonly teaches that certain people can become sacrificially alienable: slaves, war captives, condemned criminals, severe oath-breakers, and others placed under recognized forfeitures may be claimed by a ruler, owner, victor, temple, or lawful authority. Different cults disagree about the exact boundaries, and such classifications are fertile ground for political manipulation.

    The scale of Devi slavery means that routine sacrificial systems draw overwhelmingly from people already denied ordinary social protection. A free artisan, merchant, soldier, clerk, or respectable householder usually does not fear random seizure from the street. The more characteristic fear is that a powerful enemy may strip away the protections of caste, patronage, reputation, freedom, or legal standing and push the victim into a category the temples can lawfully claim.

    Prosperity and Favor¶

    Asurabalah widely treats visible success as evidence of effective divine favor. Wealth, victory, health, fertility, beauty, office, magical accomplishment, or supernatural transformation may all be cited as proof that a person or institution has cultivated the right patronage.

    This logic is highly selective. A merchant may regard his own fortune as a sacred reward while dismissing a rival's wealth as inheritance, theft, corruption, political favoritism, or the false blessing of an inferior god. Failure is equally flexible: poverty, illness, defeat, or infertility may be blamed on insufficient devotion, pollution, poor offerings, rival interference, or bad judgment—or presented as proof that the sufferer urgently needs a temple's help.

    Offerings and obedience can therefore be preached as investments in future favor. Success justifies hierarchy after the fact, while competition over whose prosperity is legitimate reinforces division between households, castes, cults, and patrons.

    Asura and Sacred Power¶

    The supernatural apex of the modern system is the Asura: a human transformed through cumulative Diabolic corruption until body and soul are no longer merely human in condition.

    Asura are treated as living proof that relationship with the Great Demon Gods can transform mortal nature. Their bodies inspire sincere awe as well as fear. The Raj of Devi is an Asura, and his transformed condition is part of the sacred logic of kingship rather than a private magical accomplishment.

    Deliberate pursuit of Asura status is an elite religious ambition rather than a universal goal. Powerful priests, rulers, generals, champions, and Diabolists may seek it, but there is no single accepted path. Different cults emphasize different vows, sacrifices, enhancements, victories, possessions, and dark accomplishments.

    Because a new Asura can become a political rival, advanced Diabolic enhancement is closely watched. Temple sponsorship, office, royal patronage, licenses, and political protection may distinguish lawful aspiration from treasonous accumulation of supernatural power.

    Possession, Corruption, and Sanctioned Diabolism¶

    Asurabalah does not treat every demonic mark or corrupting influence as equally shameful. What matters institutionally is whether the relationship is sanctioned, controlled, useful, and politically legible.

    Some cults prize disciplined bargaining and retained self-command. Others glorify frenzy, surrender, or temporary domination by a patron. Permanent possession is not the universal priestly ideal, and temporary overshadowing or bounded inhabitation remains a dangerous specialist practice rather than the ordinary condition of every priest.

    Major temples therefore maintain exorcists, demonologists, wardens, purification specialists, and other experts capable of recognizing and countering dangerous possession or contamination. Their knowledge is genuinely useful—and their authority is easily abused against rivals, dissidents, or inconvenient visionaries.

    The Asura state heavily restricts unsanctioned Diabolism. An independent summoner or pact-maker can accumulate magical power, followers, wealth, or transformative capacity outside the established hierarchy. Devi laws against unauthorized Diabolism can therefore resemble the anti-Diabolist laws of foreign realms while serving the opposite purpose: protecting the monopoly of lawful Diabolism.

    Law, Patronage, and Religious Bureaucracy¶

    Lawful Diabolism is a privilege rather than a universal private right. Dangerous practitioners may require temple sponsorship, official credentials, local presentation, guarantors, ritual permissions, or direct protection from an Asura patron.

    Such rules are not administered through one clean legal code. Rajdom law, royal decrees, temple privileges, caste rules, cult precedent, local custom, patronage, licenses, and exceptional grants overlap constantly. A practitioner may be recognized by one authority and prohibited by another.

    This bureaucratic complexity is politically useful. Bribery, gifts, selective enforcement, jurisdictional challenges, delayed filings, disputed seals, contradictory precedents, and strategic appeals give rulers and institutions leverage over one another. A sponsor may also be held answerable for the dangerous acts of a client, giving temples an incentive to police the Diabolists they empower.

    Household Religion and Older Devi Traditions¶

    For most Devi, Asurabalah has been added to an older pluralistic religious world rather than replacing it. A household may attend a Great Demon God festival, seek temple healing, maintain an ancestor shrine, consult a local spirit-worker, preserve an old birth custom, and make offerings to different powers for different needs without seeing those acts as contradictory.

    Ancestor rites remain especially rooted in households and lineages. Domestic shrines, healers, midwives, old ritual specialists, village sacred places, and protective customs may survive openly so long as they remain politically weak. The Asura order becomes more suspicious when such institutions accumulate land, wealth, followers, schools, or connections across multiple communities.

    This survival of older practice is not the same thing as an organized underground resistance. It is a broad religious substrate from which many local traditions, evasions, and later reform movements can arise.

    Death and the Afterlife¶

    Asurabalah has captured many institutions around death without erasing older ancestor religion. Temples may provide corpse preparation, purification, spirit protection, funerary arbitration, magical assistance, and prestigious rites. Basic protection may be customary or cheap; extraordinary cases can impose heavy debt on surviving kin.

    Certain dead may fall under explicit temple claims: sacrificial victims, condemned persons, high initiates, or people who vowed their remains. Such claims are bounded and often contested rather than applying automatically to every corpse.

    There is no single settled Asurabalah doctrine of the afterlife. Different cults promise different forms of patronage, elevation, protection, punishment, service, or privileged remembrance after death, while older Devi beliefs about ancestors remain widespread.

    Holy Time and Festivals¶

    Asurabalah has only a small common state-religious calendar. Most sacred time belongs to particular cults, Rajdoms, cities, occupations, lineages, households, and older seasonal traditions.

    Public festivals can be eagerly anticipated. Markets, games, food distributions, healing, marriages, divination, amnesties, debt relief, and public spectacle may all accompany major observances. The same festivals renew oaths, display hierarchy, advertise cult power, distribute patronage, recruit clients, and identify dependents.

    Human sacrifice appears at some major state and cult observances but does not define every festival. Devi sacred time is crowded rather than uniform: family custom, caste, employer, temple, patron cult, local ruler, and state may all make competing demands on the same person.

    Religious Economy¶

    Major temples are landholders and commercial institutions as well as sacred establishments. They may control estates, farms, workshops, warehouses, rental property, markets, credit, pilgrimage lodging, ritual monopolies, custody rights, and valuable exemptions.

    There is no universal Asurabalah treasury. Temples compete for donors, pilgrims, debtors, clients, captives, estates, licenses, and royal favor. Some act as major creditors or repositories of wealth; others depend more heavily on land, patronage, service fees, or state grants.

    The Raj and subordinate Asura prefer powerful temples that remain dependent. Audits, confiscations, privilege reviews, exemptions, jurisdictional disputes, and patronage can reward useful cults and weaken institutions becoming too independent.

    The result is a hierarchy of extraction and protection. Households depend on temples; minor establishments seek backing from greater ones; major cult institutions seek Asura patrons; regional Asura operate within the Raj's wider order. Wealth, service, information, loyalty, and supernatural capacity tend upward, while recognition, privileges, licenses, protection, and opportunities flow downward. Ambition is encouraged so long as advancement strengthens the structure rather than escaping it.

    Accommodation, Evasion, and Resistance¶

    Most resistance to Asurabalah is selective rather than revolutionary. Households hide assets, dispute debts, use an old healer, move a threatened relative, bribe an official, seek a rival temple's protection, conceal a shrine, or exploit contradictory jurisdictions while continuing to participate in the wider religious order.

    Sincere believers may resist abuses in Asurabalah's own language: a debt is fraudulent, a sacrifice unlawful, a governor falsely favored, or a rival temple's claim invalid. The regime's internal competition frequently creates shelter for the people it oppresses. One temple may protect a fugitive because doing so embarrasses another; an Asura may tolerate an illegal healer because the healer keeps a district stable.

    More explicit anti-Diabolist resistance also exists, but it is fragmented. Traditionalists, political rebels, dissident cults, ascetics, illegal sorcerers, and foreign-backed enemies of the Asura order need not share goals or methods, and opposition does not automatically make them virtuous.

    The Great Demon Gods¶

    The major cult powers of Asurabalah are collectively called the Great Demon Gods. The category is religious and political rather than a claim that the beings form a tidy objective pantheon. Their cults compete, overlap, form alliances, and tell incompatible stories about one another.

    Jivabandha the Fleshweaver¶

    Domains: Creation through bondage, illusion, possession, bodily reshaping, prideful dominion.

    Jivabandha creates, repairs, improves, and transforms living things through sacrificial rites and demonic power, but what he makes is never free. His cult teaches that perfected flesh, restored bodies, altered identities, and created servants remain bound by hidden obligations or direct supernatural ownership.

    In the standard Devi guise he appears as a serene or beautiful humanoid seated before a vast loom of ribs, weaving bodies, masks, beasts, servants, and perfected forms from the lives of the sacrificed. Completed creations remain attached to the loom by nearly invisible strands. His eyes are polished obsidian: black, reflective, and depthless.

    His priests speak naturally of bargains as threads, obligations as knots, enthralled servants as loom-bound, and apostates as those who have slipped the loom.

    Sanchayapala the Warden¶

    Domains: Preservation through hoarding, decay, gluttony, imprisonment, enforced hierarchy, arrested change.

    Sanchayapala teaches that valuable things should not be released. Wealth, food, offices, bodies, prisoners, debts, ancestral privilege, and power are safest when retained where authority has placed them.

    His Devi image is an immense, nearly motionless jailer seated behind a succession of nested gates. Keys, seals, and locking bars are embedded in his flesh, and he appears swollen with things stored within him. His temples commonly combine the functions of prison, granary, treasury, ossuary, and repository.

    Some of his sacrifices are sealed alive in temple cisterns or great jars so that their life and power are symbolically kept rather than allowed to pass away.

    Unmada the Ruin-Dancer¶

    Domains: Destruction and renewal through agony, lust, frenzy, ecstatic violence.

    Unmada teaches that nothing can become new until its present form has been broken. The doctrine makes destruction itself a sacrament and turns every restraint into a possible shell to be shattered.

    The Ruin-Dancer appears as a tall, beautiful, unsettling figure encased in layer after layer of painted clay skin. As the dance intensifies, each shell cracks and falls away to reveal another body beneath—older, younger, male, female, wounded, perfect, or monstrous. Broken masks are a common cult symbol.

    His worship is associated with orgiastic excess, self-mutilation, ritual demolition, mass sacrifice, and ecstatic violence.

    Lobhajani the Blood Mother¶

    Domains: Wealth through exploitation, greed, envy, maternal devouring.

    Lobhajani teaches that prosperity must be fed. A household, merchant house, caste, temple, or kingdom becomes rich because something else has been consumed for it.

    Her Devi guise is a magnificently dressed maternal figure with a flawless golden face. Her torso is hollow, and coins, jewels, and living human hearts circulate visibly within it. She cradles an empty swaddling cloth as though forever waiting for another child—or another fortune—to be placed in her care.

    Offerings include victims buried alive with treasure. The Golden Brotherhood near Shari is a distinctive Lobhajani institution whose continuing challenge and great wealth have long affected the regional economy. Its peculiar institutions should not be treated as universal features of Lobhajani worship.

    Sarwatal the Whispering Serpent¶

    Domains: Forbidden knowledge, deception, wind sorcery.

    Sarwatal is depicted in Devi as a serpentine female figure carrying a grotesque stringed instrument. Her cult pursues knowledge through demonic whispers, torture, plague, deception, and sorcery. The image is a Devi cultic guise rather than proof of an immutable cosmic body.

    Sarwatal's establishment dominates Jalaska, presently under the priest Umoja Watsala, who also controls the city's Serpent Warrior Lodge as a praetorian instrument.

    Durogchit the Skull Reaper¶

    Domains: War and death, victory, appetite, consumed strength.

    Durogchit is represented in Devi as a many-armed warrior standing atop skulls. She is associated with battlefield violence, bone ritual, demonic war-beasts, supernatural courage, blood-addled warriors, trophy-taking, domination, and the appropriation or consumption of an enemy's strength. This is her established Devi cultic guise, not necessarily an objective body recognized everywhere in the world.

    Her cult claims a special place in the history of modern Devi because Durogchit was the first Great Demon God openly invoked as an ally during the Dragon War. The first bargain at Shari demanded captured enemy soldiers rather than Shari's own population. That apparently limited price helped corrupt older captive-taking and prestige-warrior traditions into the large sacrificial systems of later centuries.

    Dahakrodha the Painted Berserker¶

    Domains: Rebellion, wrath, the sun.

    Dahakrodha is an outlaw Great Demon God whose worship is illegal within much of the Asura order. Her followers are drawn heavily from people brutalized by the existing hierarchy and turn ritual violence against priests, officials, and established sacred institutions.

    Her Devi guise is an enormous warrior woman covered from head to foot in bands of red, white, yellow, and black war-paint. A ragged corona of hammered copper blades surrounds her head, broken chain links hang from her belt, and she carries a huge notched axe said to have been made from a sacrificial implement stolen from a lawful Demon-God temple.

    Her solar aspect is the merciless noonday sun: exposure, heat, revelation, and rage that cannot remain hidden. Her cult's danger lies in the proposition that because the established order deserves what it does to its victims, anything done to overthrow it is justified.

    Zanjirani the Chain-Bearer¶

    Domains: Cunning, slavery, fate, hunting, assassination.

    Zanjirani teaches that everyone is already chained by birth, promises, fear, love, debt, fate, or hierarchy. Wisdom lies in discovering which link to pull.

    Her Devi guise is a tall veiled huntress wrapped in a seemingly endless iron chain. Lengths pass through her wrists, throat, and ribs without wounds; others disappear upward into darkness or downward into the earth. Every link bears a tiny name. Blood-red star maps cover her body.

    She is invoked by hunters and assassins. Her ritual hunts release enslaved victims only to pursue and torture them, presenting apparent freedom as a cruel demonstration that every path was already anticipated.

    Garajvanchak the Laughing Storm¶

    Domains: Thunder, debauchery, murder, betrayal, conspiracy.

    Garajvanchak is especially attractive to ambitious courtiers, heirs, generals, and rivals. His cult teaches that every oath lasts only until circumstances change and that a storm proves its strength by toppling whatever previously stood highest.

    His Devi guise is an elegantly dressed courtier whose face appears unsettlingly like someone the viewer trusts. His robes drip rain even indoors. He carries a jeweled drinking cup in one hand and conceals a knife in the other. His laughter sounds like distant thunder, and when he is wounded rain runs upward from the cut.

    His worship joins intoxication, conspiracy, assassination, violent elite turnover, and ritual killings in which victims are chained in exposed places and left to die beneath the open sky.

    Angarvriddha the Cinder King¶

    Domains: Fire, annihilation, domination.

    Angarvriddha teaches possession through destruction: what is merely conquered may someday be reclaimed, but what is reduced to ash can never belong to anyone else. Branding marks ownership; burning is the highest expression of domination.

    His Devi guise is a terribly ancient man whose body resembles charred wood or burned flesh. His eyes glow like furnace mouths. Bands of black iron have been heated and fused permanently around his limbs and torso, and he sits upon a throne of vitrified stone, melted weapons, and fused coins.

    His priests are pyromancers associated with branding, scarification, and mass burning. The cult claims Angarvriddha is the oldest of the Great Demon Gods and once ruled the others, but Asurabalah contains many incompatible sacred histories and this claim should not be treated as settled cosmological fact.

    Adventure Pressure¶

    Asurabalah most often becomes dangerous through a claim: a temple claims a debt, a corpse, a slave, a vow, a privilege, a license, a property, or jurisdiction over a person or rite. Such claims can be fought with swords and magic, but also with witnesses, patronage, contradictory precedents, rival temples, stolen records, old family rights, and the competing interests of Asura rulers.

    This makes rival institutions usable but compromised allies. A corrupt official may invalidate an unlawful sacrifice; another cult may rescue a victim for its own reasons; an illegal Diabolist may be the only person able to stop a possession. Devi remains dangerous precisely because it is habitable enough for people to build lives worth protecting inside it.
