    Easterling Faith¶

    Easterling Faith is a western convenience, not the native name of a single religion. The peoples of the Khorun share no universal church, creed, pantheon, priesthood, holy book, or standardized shamanic tradition. What they possess is an old family of related religious habits: ways of understanding how human beings live among spirits, ancestors, the dead, animals, places, guests, oath-partners, celestial powers, and communities whose relationships can carry real obligations and dangers.

    A traveler moving across Eorania may therefore recognize a family resemblance without ever encountering the same rite twice. One community may honor a burial mound, another a recurring camp, another a river crossing, another a herd road. Heaven may loom over the politics of an eastern khan while an old western house speaks more readily of ancestors and inherited obligation. Local practice matters more than any supposed universal Easterling theology.

    Relationship and Obligation¶

    Khorun religious life is usually concerned less with systematic doctrine than with right relationship. Hospitality, reciprocal gifts, offerings, oaths, remembrance, taboo, cleansing, marriage, adoption, burial, seasonal observance, and the proper use of places and animals can all carry religious weight.

    Guest Right is particularly serious. Receiving someone properly can place host and guest under obligations that are social, moral, and supernatural at once. Oaths of alliance, service, peace, marriage, adoption, or reconciliation may likewise become more than promises when genuinely constituted and witnessed.

    Offerings are common but highly variable. Food, drink, crafted objects, animal products, livestock, valuables, labor, abstinence, and other gifts may be used to thank, honor, appease, compensate, host, or negotiate with powers and relationships. Animal sacrifice is widespread in some regions and uncommon in others. There is no universal Easterling sacrificial calendar or preferred victim.

    Human sacrifice is known among some Khorun traditions and is neither universal nor confined to hidden demon cults. It is a grave and exceptionally potent act whose circumstances, legitimacy, and frequency vary enormously. Some traditions distinguish sharply between a person who genuinely offers their own life and a victim taken unwillingly. Long contact with Atharism has led many Sigyn communities in particular to abandon human sacrifice, though strong traditionalists and extraordinary funerary rites may preserve it.

    The Dead and Sacred Places¶

    The Khorun generally agree that death must be handled properly, but not on what becomes of every dead person. Ancestors, remembered founders, dangerous dead, graves, names, relics, burial grounds, and old obligations remain important without producing one common doctrine of the afterlife.

    This concern makes sacred geography compatible with a mobile life. Burial mounds, cemeteries, springs, rivers, passes, camps, assembly grounds, herd roads, hunt sites, crossings, old battlefields, mountains, and towns can all become spiritually important through repeated use and memory. A place need not contain a temple to be holy, and several peoples may maintain different legitimate relationships with the same place.

    Death by violence, dishonor, broken oath, abandonment, or improper burial is widely feared as especially dangerous. So too can be the deaths of exceptionally powerful or celebrated people, whose relationships and memory do not simply vanish with the body.

    Spirits and Religious Specialists¶

    Westerners commonly call Khorun religious specialists shamans, but the term conceals more than it explains. Spirit-mediators certainly exist, yet a community may also recognize healers, diviners, funerary guides, dream-readers, weather-workers, sacrificers, sacred poets, genealogists, omen-readers, hunt specialists, household ritual authorities, curse-breakers, or political advisers as distinct callings.

    Some possess obvious supernatural gifts; many do not. A respected genealogist, oath-witness, lamenter, sacrificer, or household elder may exercise genuine religious authority through knowledge, standing, precedent, and recognized relationship rather than overt magic. Even among practitioners who display supernatural power, outwardly similar offices may conceal very different methods.

    Most people do not require a specialist for ordinary religious life. Families and communities make routine offerings, remember their dead, receive guests, name children, arrange marriages, observe seasonal customs, cleanse themselves after dangerous events, and respond to ordinary omens on their own. Specialists become more important when a problem is obscure, dangerous, politically consequential, or clearly supernatural.

    Dreams, animal behavior, weather, celestial events, illness, fire, and chance occurrences may all be read as signs, but Khorun divination is neither infallible nor uniform. Most dreams are only dreams. Specialists can disagree, misread a sign, exaggerate their certainty, or simply lie.

    Animals and the Living Landscape¶

    Khorun traditions commonly treat animals as spiritually consequential without sharing one doctrine of animal spirits or totems. Horses, livestock, wild herds, predators, game animals, and scavengers acquire different meanings according to livelihood and history.

    Raptors are among the few recurring symbols across a very wide region. Eagles, hawks, falcons, vultures, and similar birds may be associated with sky, sight, hunting, predation, death, omen, or particular local powers. Falconry itself is not universal. In some cultures or noble houses it possesses great religious prestige; elsewhere raptors matter without ever being trained by humans.

    Holy Time¶

    There is no single Easterling liturgical year. Khorun holy time grows from the actual rhythms of life: the Moon and stars, weather and seasons, migration, wintering, planting and harvest, river movement, herd fertility, communal hunts, trade, warfare, assemblies, funerary remembrance, and local history.

    Some observances recur on fixed anniversaries, others by lunar or celestial reckoning, others when people return to a particular place, and still others only when an ecological or political event occurs. Imported religions add further calendars without necessarily erasing older ones.

    The Sigyn¶

    Among the Sigyn, religion is deeply entangled with ancestral landscapes and inherited obligation. Burial mounds, cemeteries, house graves, old estates, battle sites, springs, groves, genealogy, lament, and inherited objects can carry centuries of accumulated meaning. Great mounds are not merely tombs: they may anchor house memory, inheritance claims, meetings, oaths, pilgrimage, hauntings, and disputes over who truly belongs to an ancestral line.

    Horses are particularly important in elite religious life, where celebrated animals may become bound to house identity, warrior reputation, gifts, burial, and memory. Some houses also cultivate distinctive relationships with raptors or falconry, but these are aristocratic and regional traditions rather than a Sigyn-wide cult.

    Sigyn ancestry is not supposed to excuse failure toward the living. A house that boasts of ancient founders while betraying guests, abandoning dependents, breaking oaths, or neglecting its dead can turn its own sacred claims into accusations against itself. Feud, compensation, reconciliation, and the settlement of violent death therefore possess religious as well as political importance.

    Centuries of contact with Atharism have produced conversion, intermarriage, argument, and syncretism. Many Atharite Sigyn retain some ancestral commemorations and local customs, while older burial traditions can sit uneasily beside Atharite cremation. There is no single settlement of that tension across the western Khorun.

    The Chulkas¶

    Among the Chulkas, mobility is not a departure from sacred order but one of its normal forms. Recurring camps, wintering places, graves, water, pasture, routes, and assembly grounds create a moving religious geography. Genealogy, song, remembered graves, ancestral possessions, and reputation allow ancestry itself to travel when a clan loses territory.

    The camp is an ordinary sacred center, and a confederation can acquire spiritual identity through shared oaths, standards, feasts, victories, graves, songs, and remembered leaders without becoming a church. Hospitality, alliance, adoption, patronage, and generosity carry exceptional weight in a society where strangers must repeatedly become allies and followers.

    A successful leader is expected to distribute livestock, booty, food, protection, pasture access, gifts, and opportunity. Fortune matters, but it is contested: military success may suggest favor or right relationship, while failure may be blamed on broken obligations, curse, poor judgment, or simple incompetence.

    Heaven and the open sky are especially prominent in many Chulkas traditions, though without one universal theology. Weather, celestial signs, herd fertility, and seasonal fortune consequently receive close attention. The present Khazgui-driven displacement is also a religious crisis, breaking old migration circuits and separating communities from graves, recurring camps, known spirits, and inherited places.

    The Khazgui¶

    The rise of Khazgui power under Hu Khan is transforming eastern Khorun religion as quickly as it transforms politics. Repeated conquest has encouraged increasingly comprehensive claims that Heaven favors the new order, but neither Khazgui belief nor outside observation has settled exactly what that means.

    Hu Khan's supporters can point to victory, restored roads, disciplined service, tribute, incorporated peoples, and an apparently inexhaustible sequence of successes. Others attribute those successes to skill, fortune, sorcery, ancestry, ruthless organization, or powers less easily named. His rumored Oni ancestry remains rumor; religion provides arguments about Hu Khan, not proof of what he is.

    Service itself is acquiring sacred political weight. Oaths, standards, ranks, offices, rewards, and repeated recognition bind soldiers, administrators, translators, craftsmen, subject warriors, and other useful people into an expanding order that can cross older ancestry. Khazgui rulers generally have practical reasons to preserve the gods, ancestors, temples, and specialists of incorporated peoples when these do not threaten obedience, making conquest a powerful engine of religious mixture.

    The court consequently attracts diviners, astrologers, healers, spirit-workers, sorcerers, foreign priests, and ambitious occultists of many kinds. Songs, standards, battlefields, possessions, and stories already gather around Hu Khan himself, but what becomes of that growing cult of memory—and whether his victories truly constitute a mandate of Heaven—remains unanswered.

    The Uldat¶

    Uldat religion reflects a world of rivers, towns, markets, crossings, farms, mobile warriors, and foreign travelers. Confluences, harbors, bridges, wells, cemeteries, islands, markets, walls, gates, and civic meeting places can accumulate overlapping sacred claims. The same river may be approached differently by boatmen, farmers, merchants, warrior households, foreigners, and old local communities.

    Layered belonging is therefore ordinary. A household may preserve ancestral and river customs while also participating in Atharism, a southern tradition, a foreign merchant cult, or another imported practice. Uldat religious specialists often become brokers between systems rather than guardians of one exclusive orthodoxy.

    Ancient Khorun concern with oath and hospitality has adapted readily to urban and commercial life. Contracts, partnerships, loans, shipping ventures, employment, guild obligations, merchant guarantees, inns, and civic safe-conduct can acquire religious seriousness as well as legal force.

    Uldat funerary practice is correspondingly plural. River towns must find ways to deal with drowned travelers, foreigners, epidemic victims, executed criminals, unknown bodies, and the unrecovered dead as well as their own ancestors. Trade brings new religions continually, but adoption is often layered rather than an immediate exchange of one complete faith for another.

    The Ordai¶

    Among the Ordai, religion is inseparable from the wild-herd landscape. Herd movement, communal hunting, seasonal aggregation, predators, weather, migration corridors, springs, hunt sites, old camps, and recurring gathering grounds structure relationships between people and country.

    The Ordai are not passive custodians of untouched nature. They hunt intensively, manipulate herd movement, compete with predators, and use the land pragmatically. Religious concern falls instead on correct taking, distribution, avoidance of waste, continuity of return, and the limits of human claims. A successful communal hunt therefore joins sacred and political life: scouting, killing, butchery, allocation, generosity, need, prestige, hospitality, and household obligation are parts of the same undertaking.

    Some bands, families, lineages, or specialists maintain unusually strong relationships with particular animals, but not every Ordai possesses a totem. Predators such as wolves, great cats, raptors, ravens, and vultures can be rivals, teachers, dangerous powers, fellow hunters, or omens according to local tradition.

    Ordai funerary customs characteristically favor elevated exposure—platforms, scaffolds, trees, high stone shelves, and related forms—though practice varies. Wind, sun, raptors, scavengers, and predators may participate in the body's return to the landscape rather than automatically being understood as desecration.

    Authority is as plural in religion as in politics. Hunt leaders, warriors, diplomats, elders, healers, spirit-workers, and keepers of memory need not be the same people. Sacred places likewise need not be owned: a herd bottleneck, exposure ground, spring, or aggregation site may impose obligations on those who use it without becoming private property.

    Conversion and Religious Change¶

    The Khorun have never been religiously sealed from their neighbors. Atharism, southern ascetic and mystical traditions, The Way of the Dragon, foreign cults, subject religions, merchants, captives, healers, wandering teachers, and conquerors all carry practices and ideas across Eorania.

    Change is often layered rather than binary. A convert may adopt a new theology while continuing ancestral remembrance, local spirit customs, household offerings, funerary habits, or seasonal observances. A ruler may sponsor foreign priests for political reasons without transforming every household. A conquering people may appropriate a subject community's sacred legitimacy rather than abolish it.

    This does not make the Khorun uniformly tolerant. Borrowing can coexist with persecution, reform, shrine destruction, accusations of corruption, rival specialists, political manipulation, or demands for exclusive allegiance. Older Devi-derived customs are not automatically Diabolical, while genuine Diabolism does appear among ambitious, desperate, or corrupted practitioners and rulers. Because Demons are real, accusations of Diabolism can be both genuine warnings and useful political weapons.

    Religious argument among the Khorun is therefore usually argument within a supernatural world, not disbelief in one. People dispute whether an omen was genuine, whether a specialist is competent, whether an ancestor's claim still binds, whether a foreign rite is safe, whether a ruler's victories prove favor, or whether an old obligation has become destructive. Reform usually produces another religious arrangement rather than a secular one.
